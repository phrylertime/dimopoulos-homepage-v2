The Retainer Process

You have already signed thisYou are reading this because you have retained us, so nothing on this page asks anything of you. It is here so you understand what you signed, what it obligates each of us to do, and the court rules the firm is held to in getting it right.

Before we can act as your attorneys, New York law requires a written retainer agreement between you and the firm. It is a contract, and we want you to understand it before you sign it.

What the retainer agreement is

The retainer agreement defines the scope of our representation, how our fees and disbursements are billed, and the rights and responsibilities that run in both directions. In a matrimonial matter the agreement covers your case through the entry of a final judgment or settlement. Appeals, post-judgment enforcement, and modification proceedings fall outside that scope and would require a separate agreement if they arise.

↗

The rules we are held toRetainer agreements in divorce and family matters are not left to the lawyer's discretion. New York regulates them by court rule, and you can read the rule yourself: Procedure for Attorneys in Domestic Relations Matters, 22 NYCRR Part 1400. Among other things it requires a written agreement signed by both of us and filed with the court, prohibits nonrefundable retainers, requires billing at least every sixty days, and sets out your right to arbitrate a fee dispute. Everything in our agreement is built to comply with it.

Attached to every retainer agreement is the Statement of Client's Rights and Responsibilities required by the rules of the Appellate Division (22 NYCRR § 1210.1). Read it. It describes, among other things, your right to courteous treatment, to itemized bills at reasonable intervals, to prompt responses to your questions, and to make the final decision on whether your case settles. That last decision is always yours, not ours.

What you are retaining

The engagement is with the firm as an entity rather than with one attorney exclusively, which is standard and which the agreement says plainly. What that language does not convey is how the firm actually works: Gus personally handles every significant aspect of your representation, including the court appearances, the hearings, the depositions, the negotiations, and the trial. Work is delegated to associates, paralegals, and law clerks precisely so that his hours go to the parts of your case that decide it, and so that assembling documents and organizing discovery is billed at a fraction of his rate. Delegation here is a cost control, not a handoff.

How the retainer and billing work

The retainer is an advance payment that is applied against our hourly time charges and out-of-pocket disbursements as your case proceeds. It is not a flat fee and it is not a prediction of what your case will cost. Each month you receive an itemized bill showing the date and nature of every service, who performed it, and the time charged, along with any disbursements such as court filing fees, court reporters, transcripts, and process servers.

Outside experts are common in matrimonial litigation: appraisers, forensic accountants, and mental health professionals among them. Their fees are separate from ours and are your responsibility, though here too the court can direct your spouse to share or carry the cost in appropriate cases.

If anything on a bill looks wrong to you, raise it right away. The agreement gives you a window of twenty days to object, and prompt questions get prompt fixes.

Ground Rules That Protect You

A handful of habits, adopted on day one, will protect your privilege, your evidence, and your position. These are not suggestions. They are the rules we ask every client to live by while a case is pending.

Secure your communications

Attorney-client privilege can be lost if your confidential communications are accessible to third parties. Never use a work email account for anything related to your case. Never use a shared device, or any device or account your spouse has access to or could gain access to. Set a fresh, unique password on the phone and email account you will use with us. If you have any doubt about whether an account is clean, say so and we will set up a new one with you.

Turn on two-factor authentication everywhere it is offered: your email, your Apple or Google account, your banking, and the client portal. Use your own phone as the second factor, never a shared number or a device your spouse can unlock. Two-factor authentication is the single cheapest protection available to you, and it defeats most of the ways a curious spouse gets into an account.

Your iPhone knows more than you think

Apple's ecosystem is built for family sharing, which is exactly the problem. A shared Apple ID, a Family Sharing group, an old iPad the kids use that is still signed into your account, a Mac that syncs your messages, a shared photo album, saved passwords in a shared Keychain, location visible through Find My: any one of these can hand your spouse a live feed of your texts, photos, passwords, and whereabouts without anything being "hacked."

In your first week with us: change your Apple ID password and your phone passcode, review the list of devices signed into your Apple ID and remove every one you do not physically control, turn off location sharing with your spouse, leave any shared Family group photo streams quietly alone until we discuss them, and check which devices receive your text messages. We will walk you through this at the onboarding conference; it takes twenty minutes and it matters more than almost anything else on this page. The same logic applies to Google accounts and Android devices.

Send documents securely, never by email

You will have two secure ways to send us documents, and you can use whichever suits what you are sending. Both are encrypted, both are access-controlled, and both land directly in your matter file where your legal team can work with them. Email attachments are none of those things: they can be intercepted, forwarded, or misaddressed, they sit unencrypted in inboxes that get compromised, and they leave no reliable record of what was sent when. In cases where a spouse may have visibility into an email account, and we handle many of those, this is not merely tidier. It is a safety measure. Bank statements, tax returns, photographs, anything at all: it goes through one of these two channels.

The first is your client portal, which runs through Clio, the system that manages our practice. You will receive an invitation by email when your matter opens, and it works in any browser and on your phone. It takes up to twenty documents at a time, which suits the steady flow of a case: a few statements here, a school record there. If you install the Clio app, you can photograph a document and have the app turn it into a clean PDF, which is far better than emailing yourself a picture. The second is a secure link through our Microsoft system, which we provide for large volumes. When you are sending three years of bank statements rather than three documents, use that one.

Beyond choosing between them, two habits carry you. Name files plainly, because "Chase checking statements 2024" beats "scan0001," and send as you go rather than saving everything for one avalanche. Treat your portal login the way you treat your email login: your own password, two-factor authentication on, never on a shared device, and never shared with anyone, including family members trying to help.

Forward everything, answer nothing

If your spouse, your spouse's lawyer, or anyone else contacts you about the case, do not engage on substance. Forward the communication to us the same day. What feels like a harmless reply can become an exhibit.

Stay off social media

While the case is pending, do not post about your spouse, the case, the court, the firm, or any witness. Do not delete, deactivate, or alter any existing account or content without talking to us first, because social media content is evidence and destroying it can hurt you far more than the content itself. Ask friends and family not to post about the matter either. Assume everything you have ever posted will be read by your spouse's lawyer, because it will be.

Recording conversations: ask us first

New York is a one-party consent state. Under the Penal Law's eavesdropping provisions (Penal Law §§ 250.00, 250.05), you may lawfully record a conversation you are party to, without telling the other person. What you may never do is record a conversation you are not a party to: planting a recorder in the house, tapping a phone, or leaving a device running to capture your spouse talking to someone else is felony eavesdropping, and the recording is worthless as evidence anyway. Recordings involving the children raise special rules of their own. The safe practice is simple: before you record anything, or if you already have recordings, talk to Gus. Some recordings win cases; others create criminal exposure for the person who made them.

Preserve documents and records

Keep every financial record, message, photo, and document that touches the marriage, the children, or the finances, even ones that seem unhelpful. Litigation carries a duty to preserve evidence, and gaps in records invite worse inferences than the records themselves.

Gathering evidence the right way

There is a bright line in evidence gathering, and staying on the right side of it is the difference between evidence we can use and evidence that damages you. You may copy and photograph what you lawfully have access to: joint tax returns, statements that arrive at the house, records of joint accounts, documents kept in shared spaces of the home, the contents of a shared safe. Make copies early, inventory valuables, and photograph anything that might later go missing.

You may not log into your spouse's email, phone, or accounts, guess their passwords, use their saved logins or Face ID, install tracking software, or go through a briefcase or office you have no right to access. Unauthorized access to accounts is a crime under both state and federal law, it can poison otherwise winnable issues, and anything it produces is usually unusable. If information exists that we need and you cannot lawfully reach it, that is what discovery, subpoenas, and forensic experts are for. Tell us what you believe exists and where, and let us get it the right way.

If you think you are being monitored

Spyware on phones, trackers on cars, and compromised accounts appear in matrimonial cases far more often than people expect, particularly in high-conflict ones. If your spouse seems to know things they should not know, say so at the first meeting. The firm works with the best digital forensic experts in the field, examiners who can inspect phones, computers, vehicles, and accounts, document what they find in a form a court will accept, and clean your devices without destroying the evidence. Do not try to find or remove spyware yourself first; how it is discovered and preserved determines whether it becomes leverage in your case.

Tell us the bad facts first

Every case has them. Bad facts we know about get managed; bad facts that surface for the first time in your spouse's papers, or at your deposition, do damage that is hard to undo. Nothing you tell Gus will shock him, and all of it is privileged. If a fact is bad enough that you are dreading the conversation, that is the one to bring to him first.

Commencement & the Automatic Orders

A New York divorce begins when a summons is filed with the County Clerk and served on the other spouse. From that moment, the case exists, the clock starts, and a set of automatic court orders takes effect.

What happens

The spouse who starts the action (the plaintiff) files a summons, either alone with a notice or together with a complaint setting out the claims. The other spouse (the defendant) is served and has a limited time to appear. Since 2010, New York permits no-fault divorce: a sworn statement that the marriage has broken down irretrievably for at least six months is a sufficient ground, so cases today are almost never fought over the divorce itself. They are fought over money and children.

Does it matter who files first?

For the most part, no. Clients often worry that filing second means starting behind, or that filing first signals aggression. Neither is true. Being the plaintiff carries no meaningful legal advantage in a New York divorce: it does not affect custody, support, or how property is divided. There are occasional tactical reasons to file promptly, locking in the valuation date and triggering the automatic orders among them, and we will tell you if your case presents one. But the caption of the case does not decide it.

Filing does not mean a courtroom

Just because an action has been started does not mean anyone is standing in front of a judge. A case only gets assigned to a Justice after one side files a Request for Judicial Intervention (the RJI), and until that happens there may be no court appearances at all. Months sometimes pass between the summons and the first conference while the lawyers exchange information or explore settlement. That quiet period is normal, and often useful. When the RJI is filed, the case is assigned to a Justice of the Supreme Court who will preside over it from conference through trial, and the calendar begins.

The automatic orders

Domestic Relations Law § 236(B)(2)(b) imposes automatic orders on both spouses in every divorce. They bind the plaintiff upon filing and the defendant upon service, without either side asking for them. In broad terms: no selling, transferring, hiding, or borrowing against property outside the ordinary course; no touching retirement accounts; no running up unreasonable debt; no removing the other spouse or the children from insurance; and no changing life insurance beneficiaries. The full text you will receive with the summons is reproduced below, and it is worth reading once in its entirety.

NOTICE OF AUTOMATIC ORDERS (DRL § 236)Served with the summons in every New York matrimonial action

FAILURE TO COMPLY WITH THESE ORDERS MAY BE DEEMED A CONTEMPT OF COURT

PURSUANT TO DOMESTIC RELATIONS LAW § 236 part B, section 2, as added by chapter 72 of the laws of 2009, both you and your spouse (the parties) are bound by the following AUTOMATIC ORDERS, which shall remain in full force and effect during the pendency of the action unless terminated, modified or amended by further order of the court or upon written agreement between the parties:

Upon service of the summons in every matrimonial action, it is hereby:

1. ORDERED: Neither party shall sell, transfer, encumber, conceal, assign, remove or in any way dispose of, without consent of the other party in writing, or by order of the court, any property (including, but not limited to, real estate, personal property, cash accounts, stocks, mutual funds, bank accounts, cars and boats) individually or jointly held by the parties except in the usual course of business, for customary and usual household expenses or for reasonable attorney's fees in connection with this action.

2. ORDERED: Neither party shall transfer, encumber, assign, remove, withdraw or in any way dispose of any tax deferred funds, stocks or other assets held in any individual retirement accounts, 401(k) accounts, profit sharing plans, Keogh accounts, or any other pension or retirement account, and the parties shall further refrain from applying for or requesting the payment of retirement benefits or annuity payments of any kind, without the consent of the other party in writing, or upon further order of the court; except that any party who is already in pay status may continue to receive such payments thereunder.

3. ORDERED: Neither party shall incur unreasonable debts hereafter, including, but not limited to further borrowing against any credit line secured by the family residence, further encumbrances on any assets, or unreasonably using credit cards or cash advances against credit cards, except in the usual course of business or for customary or usual household expenses, or for reasonable attorney's fees in connection with this action.

4. ORDERED: Neither party shall cause the other party or the children of the marriage to be removed from any existing medical, hospital and dental insurance coverage, and each party shall maintain the existing medical, hospital and dental insurance coverage in full force and effect.

5. ORDERED: Neither party shall change the beneficiaries of any existing life insurance policies, and each party shall maintain the existing life insurance, automobile insurance, homeowners and renters insurance policies in full force and effect.

These orders protect the marital estate while the case is decided. Violations can be punished as contempt, and courts take them seriously. If you think your spouse has violated them, or you are unsure whether something you want to do is permitted, ask us before acting.

The Statement of Net Worth

The Statement of Net Worth is the single most important financial document in your divorce. It is a sworn, court-mandated statement of everything you earn, spend, own, and owe.

What it is

The court rules governing contested matrimonial cases (22 NYCRR § 202.16) require each spouse to prepare, exchange, and file a Statement of Net Worth on the official form, and to do so early, no later than ten days before the preliminary conference. It is signed under oath. It follows your case everywhere: judges read it before conferences, support is calculated from it, lawyers cross-examine from it at depositions and at trial.

PDF

The official court formYou can see the Statement of Net Worth form itself on the New York courts website: Net Worth Statement (NY Unified Court System). Skim it now so you know what is coming; we prepare it together, but the categories below are what it will demand.

What goes into it

The form covers five financial areas in detail: your monthly expenses, category by category, from housing and utilities to groceries, insurance, and the children's activities; your income from every source, tied to your tax returns and pay records; your assets, including real estate, bank and brokerage accounts, retirement accounts, business interests, vehicles, and valuables; your liabilities, from mortgages to credit cards to loans; and supporting details such as household members and the fees you have paid your lawyers. Your most recent tax return and pay stubs are attached.

Why accuracy matters so much

Because it is sworn, an inaccurate Statement of Net Worth is not just an embarrassment; it is impeachment material for the other side and a credibility problem with the judge that can shadow the entire case. Estimates are sometimes unavoidable, and the form allows for them, but guesses dressed up as facts are dangerous. Gus goes through every line of it with you before anything is signed.

What we need from you, within one week of engaging us

Unless we tell you sooner, deliver the following within a week of retaining us. Send everything through the portal or the secure link, both covered in the ground rules.

  • Federal and state tax returns, with all schedules and W-2s or 1099s, for the last three years
  • Your last six pay stubs, or year-to-date income records if self-employed
  • Three (3) years of statements for every bank, brokerage, and retirement account
  • Three (3) years of credit card statements and balances for every card
  • Mortgage statements, deeds, and any appraisals for real estate
  • Insurance policies: life, health, homeowners, auto
  • Your best month-by-month accounting of household expenses; we will provide a worksheet

Custody & the Parenting Track

If you have children, your divorce runs on two tracks at once. The financial track described in the surrounding sections is one. Custody and parenting time is the other, and it moves on its own schedule, starting immediately.

Come to the preliminary conference with a plan

By the preliminary conference, you need a parenting position: a proposed schedule for where the children are on school days, weekends, holidays, and vacations, and a proposal for how major decisions about education, health, and religion get made. We will build it with you. What you cannot do is arrive without one. When parents show the court that they have no framework and cannot discuss one, the court fills the vacuum: it appoints an Attorney for the Children, and in contested cases it may order a forensic custody evaluation. Both decisions are effectively out of your hands once made, both are expensive, and both put strangers between you and your children's case. A credible parenting plan, even one the other side has not yet accepted, is how you keep control of this track.

A credible parenting plan, even one the other side has not yet accepted, is how you keep control of this track.

Once an AFC is involved

The Attorney for the Children, or AFC, is a lawyer appointed by the court to represent your children, not either parent. The AFC meets with the children, speaks with both sides' counsel, and takes positions at every conference; in most cases the AFC is charged with advocating what the children actually want, not what either parent thinks is best for them. The parties typically pay the AFC's fees. Once appointed, the AFC is a permanent participant in the case, and how each parent deals with the AFC is noticed. Never coach the children before an AFC meeting, never quiz them after, and never speak to the AFC yourself without talking to us first. Children sense pressure, and AFCs are trained to spot it.

The forensic evaluation

In seriously contested custody cases, where there are allegations of mental illness, domestic violence, or addiction, the court may appoint a forensic evaluator, a psychologist or psychiatrist, to evaluate the family. The process is thorough and slow: interviews with each parent, sessions with the children, observation of each parent with the children, psychological testing, and calls with collateral sources such as teachers, pediatricians, and therapists. It typically takes months and costs tens of thousands of dollars, usually allocated between the parties. The evaluator's report goes to the court, and while it does not bind the judge, it carries real weight. If a forensic is ordered in your case, we will prepare you carefully for it; how parents present across months of evaluation matters far more than any single session.

Custody and money can settle separately

Nothing requires the two tracks to finish together. Parents can sign a custody and parenting stipulation early, even while the financial case is fully contested, and courts encourage it. Settling custody first spares the children the longest and worst part of the fight, ends the AFC's and evaluator's involvement, and, candidly, removes the temptation each side otherwise has to use the children as leverage in the financial negotiation. Where custody can be resolved sensibly, we try to resolve it early.

Gus will thoroughly prepare you for interactions with the AFC and the forensic evaluator himself.

The Preliminary Conference

The preliminary conference is your case's first appearance before the assigned judge. It is where the court takes control of the calendar and the roadmap for everything that follows gets written.

When it happens

Under the matrimonial rules, the court schedules the preliminary conference promptly after either party files a Request for Judicial Intervention. Both parties are expected to attend with their attorneys, and each side must have served and filed its Statement of Net Worth beforehand.

Preparation is the whole game

Here is a candid observation from decades of these conferences: most lawyers do not prepare enough for them. They treat the preliminary conference as a scheduling formality, show up, and let the form fill itself out. We treat it as the day the case gets framed. Before Gus walks in, he will have worked with you to identify, to the best of our shared ability at that early stage, every issue in the case, every category of documents we need from the other side, every claim we intend to make, and every expert we expect to hire: appraisers, business valuators, forensic accountants, vocational experts. The deadlines and expert provisions written into the order that day govern the case for a year or more. A side that has not thought its case through commits itself blind. We do not.

Most lawyers do not prepare enough for the preliminary conference. We treat it as the day the case gets framed.Gus Dimopoulos

How the conference actually works

Expect to spend the better part of a morning, often two to four hours, at the courthouse, most of it waiting. The Matrimonial Part calendars many cases the same day. When your case is called, the working session will be with the judge assigned to your case, which is the practice in Westchester and sometimes differs in other counties. The lawyers negotiate their way through the court's standard form order section by section: what issues are resolved, what is contested, the discovery timetable, appraisals and expert appointments, and any interim arrangements that need addressing. You will not testify and you will rarely address the court; your role is to be present, consult with us in the hallway as terms are negotiated, and make decisions. At the end, the completed stipulation is signed by counsel and so-ordered by the judge, and its deadlines become court orders, not aspirations.

Dress, demeanor, and first impressions

Assume you are being evaluated from the moment you enter the building, because you are. Judges, court attorneys, court officers, the AFC if one has been appointed, and opposing counsel all form impressions, and in a custody case those impressions matter. Dress in conservative business attire, the way you would for the most important job interview of your life. Arrive early. Keep your phone away and silenced. 06Anatomy of a Divorce

The Discovery Process

Discovery is the formal, court-supervised exchange of information between the spouses. It is usually the longest phase of a divorce, and it is where financial cases are won or lost.

The governing principle

New York's disclosure statute, CPLR article 31, requires "full disclosure of all matter material and necessary" to the case. In a divorce, that reach is long. Compulsory financial disclosure applies in every contested matrimonial action; neither spouse can refuse to open the books, and hiding the ball has consequences that range from preclusion of evidence to adverse inferences at trial.

The tools

Document demands
Written demands that the other side produce specified records: statements, tax returns, business books, contracts, messages. The workhorse of matrimonial discovery, covered in the next section.
Interrogatories
Written questions answered in writing under oath, typically used to pin down income sources, asset histories, and account inventories.
Notices to admit
Requests that the other side admit specific facts or the genuineness of documents, narrowing what has to be proven at trial.
Subpoenas to third parties
When a spouse's records are incomplete or not trusted, the records come from the source instead: banks, employers, business partners, credit card issuers.
Depositions
Sworn testimony taken before trial, covered in its own section of this guide.
Experts and appraisals
Neutral or retained experts value what cannot be read off a statement: businesses, professional practices, real estate, pensions, and in some cases a spouse's true earning capacity.

Where cash and hidden income are involved

Some cases involve a spouse whose lifestyle does not match the income being reported. Discovery is built for that problem. Lifestyle analysis, forensic accounting, third-party subpoenas, and deposition testimony under oath work together to establish what a household actually spends and what actually comes in, and courts are permitted to impute income to a spouse whose reported numbers do not add up. If this describes your case, the discipline of the document work ahead is not busywork. It is the case.

How long it takes

The preliminary conference order typically allots roughly six months for financial disclosure, and in a truly simple case that holds. In practice, cases with businesses to value, third-party subpoenas to fight over, or a spouse slow to produce run longer: a year of discovery is common in contested cases, and complex ones run beyond that. The single biggest driver of the timeline is not the court and not the lawyers. It is how completely and how quickly each side produces. One of the few schedule levers you personally control is being the side that is never the cause of delay.

What it costs, honestly

Discovery is usually the most expensive phase of a divorce short of trial, and it is where the size of your bill is decided. The cost drivers are volume (thousands of pages must be gathered, reviewed, and organized), disputes (every fight over what must be produced means letters, conferences, and sometimes motions), and experts (valuations and forensic accounting are their own budgets). We cannot quote a number in a general guide because the honest answer depends on your estate and your adversary. What we can tell you is how to keep your share down: produce completely and on time, use the portal so nothing is handled twice, batch your questions, and let our technology and support staff do the organizing that other firms bill out at attorney rates. The expensive discovery phases are almost always the ones where the parties fight about the process instead of doing it.

The Exchange of Documents

Document exchange is discovery made concrete: each side demands records, gathers its own, and produces them in an organized, numbered, and trackable form. Done well, it is methodical. Done badly, it is where the cost of a divorce gets out of control.

How the exchange works

Demands are served

Each side serves a written demand listing the categories of documents it wants, with a court-ordered deadline to respond. The categories track what you saw in the Statement of Net Worth section, extended to the other side and reaching deeper into anything contested.

You gather, we review

You collect what the demand calls for and send it through the portal or the secure link; we review everything before anything leaves the firm, assert any applicable objections and privileges, and decide what is properly withheld.

Production is numbered and served

Every produced page carries a unique identifying stamp, so both sides and the court can cite any document precisely for the rest of the case.

We audit what comes back

The other side's production is indexed against our demand, item by item. Gaps and evasions are documented in deficiency letters, and if they persist, in motions to compel.

In a high-net-worth case

Where the estate is substantial, the exchange goes far beyond bank statements. Much of the most important information comes from third parties rather than the spouse: subpoenas to banks, employers, business partners, fund administrators, and accountants, because records from the source cannot be curated by the person being examined. Where a spouse holds interests in a hedge fund or private equity firm, the analysis has its own vocabulary: partnership and operating agreements, K-1s, capital account statements, carried interest and co-investment rights, deferred and clawback-subject compensation, side letters, and the difference between what an interest pays this year and what it is actually worth. These structures are designed to be opaque, sometimes deliberately so in a divorce. Our forensic accountants and valuation experts have unwound them many times, and the discovery demands we serve in these cases are built from that experience.

Discovery disputes, and why they cost so much

When the other side does not produce, the court's rules require an escalation path. Counsel must first confer in good faith, and any discovery motion must include an affirmation that they did (22 NYCRR § 202.7). The court holds compliance conferences to police its own deadlines, and most judges require a pre-motion letter or conference before a discovery motion may even be filed. Each step of that ladder is a letter, a court appearance, or a motion, and each carries hours of attorney time. Multiply by a dozen disputed items and you understand why this phase, more than any other, is where legal fees balloon. We escalate when the value of the information justifies it and tell you plainly when it does not; some fights are worth two court appearances and some are not, and you will always know which we think yours is.

Volume is the other enemy, and we solve it with technology rather than hours. Discovery does not fade out; it is formally closed. At the final compliance conference the court certifies that disclosure is complete, and the case is then ready for the note of issue, the filing that places it on the trial calendar. Trial preparation, covered ahead, begins there.

Depositions

A deposition, called an examination before trial or EBT in New York practice, is sworn testimony given before trial. You answer the other lawyer's questions under oath, a court reporter takes down every word, and the transcript can be used at trial.

What it looks like

Depositions usually take place in a law office conference room, not a courtroom. No judge is present. You, both lawyers, and a court reporter are in the room; your spouse is entitled to attend. The other side's lawyer asks questions, we object where the rules allow, and in most instances you answer even over an objection, with the dispute preserved for the judge to resolve later. A deposition can run a few hours or a full day, and complex financial cases sometimes need more than one session.

Your spouse will be deposed too. That session is frequently one of the most productive events in the case: it is the first time the other spouse must answer our questions, under oath, on the record, with the documents in front of them.

How to be a good witness

Gus prepares you himself before you testify, and that session matters as much as the deposition itself. The core of it comes down to a few disciplines. Listen to the whole question before answering. Answer only the question asked, then stop; volunteering is how testimony goes wrong. Never guess: "I don't know" and "I don't recall" are honest, complete answers when they are true. Take your time, because the transcript does not show pauses. If you do not understand a question, say so. And tell the truth without exception, because a witness caught shading the record once is doubted on everything.

Why it matters

Deposition testimony is locked in. Contradict it at trial and the transcript is read back to you in front of the judge. Depositions therefore do more than gather information: they fix each side's story, expose the weaknesses in it, and very often move cases decisively toward settlement once both sides have seen how the witnesses hold up.

The Settlement Process

The great majority of New York divorce cases end in a written agreement rather than a verdict.

Settlement can happen at almost any point: before anything is filed, during the months of disclosure, or on the courthouse steps. The serious push usually comes later in the process, once both sides know what the other's case actually looks like and what a trial would risk. Everything in the stages that follow is building toward this, whether or not anyone says so out loud.

What the process actually looks like

Clients sometimes imagine settlement as one dramatic meeting. It is the opposite: many meetings and many calls over weeks or months. Attorney-to-attorney negotiations. Four-way conferences with both spouses and both lawyers in the room. Term sheets exchanged, marked up, and exchanged again. Court-encouraged settlement conferences. Sometimes a mediator for the last stubborn issues. Progress is rarely linear; deals come together, fall apart over one provision, and come together again. That is normal, not a bad sign.

The experts stay involved

Numbers drive matrimonial settlements, so the experts who developed the numbers stay at the table. Valuations set the range for buyouts of a business or practice. Accountants model what different support structures actually cost after tax, and what each proposal leaves each household to live on. Where the experts disagree, the negotiation is often really about whose analysis a judge would credit, and we will tell you frankly how we assess that risk.

Be honest with yourself about the toll

This phase is time-consuming and stressful in a way clients do not expect. By the time serious negotiation begins the document work is largely behind you, but now every draft that arrives asks you to make decisions about your home, your children, and your financial future, often with a deadline attached. Emotional exhaustion is a real negotiating force, and the other side may try to use it. Part of Gus's job is pacing: knowing when pressing hard wins the point and when patience does, and making sure you never sign anything because you are simply worn out. The decision to settle is always yours; our job is to make sure it is made with clear eyes.

What a settlement becomes

Agreements are papered as stipulations of settlement: sometimes one global agreement, and sometimes a custody and parenting stipulation signed separately from the financial stipulation. As the custody section explains, the two tracks can resolve at different times.

Signing is not the last step

Clients are often surprised by this. A signed stipulation of settlement resolves your case, but it does not by itself end your marriage. Once it is signed, the lawyers have to convert the case into an uncontested divorce: we prepare the proposed judgment of divorce along with the full set of uncontested papers, the findings of fact, the affirmations, the child support and case registry forms, and the rest, and submit the package to the assigned Justice for signature. Your stipulation is incorporated into the judgment and carries the same force.

It is a simple process and it does not require you to appear. But it is a real final step, and it has to be taken carefully, because the judgment is the document you will rely on for years and errors in it are tedious to fix. Expect several months of court processing time between submission and a signed judgment. Your stipulation governs your lives in the meantime, so nothing is in limbo while you wait.

Trial Preparation & the Close of Discovery

You reach this stage one of two ways. Settlement has failed outright, or, more often, negotiations are still technically alive while both sides edge toward a trial date, each waiting for the other to blink.

The game of chicken, and the mistake it breeds

That second scenario produces the biggest mistake lawyers make at this stage, and clients rarely know enough to catch it: treating the trial date as a bluff. A lawyer who believes the case will settle prepares half-heartedly, and when the deal falls through three weeks before trial, there is no recovering the lost months. The irony is that under-preparing also kills the settlement itself, because the other side can read preparation across the table, and nobody improves an offer to a lawyer who is visibly not ready to try the case. We prepare as if trial is certain from the moment the note of issue is filed. It is the only honest way to litigate, and it is also, not coincidentally, the best settlement strategy available.

What the calendar demands

The note of issue and certificate of readiness close discovery and place the case on the trial calendar. 10Anatomy of a Divorce

Trial & Judgment

If the case does not settle, it is tried to the judge. There is no jury in a New York divorce trial: the assigned Justice hears the evidence and decides custody, support, maintenance, and the division of property. And you should know before choosing this path: trial is grueling, all-encompassing, and time-consuming in ways this page will spell out plainly.

How a divorce trial runs

Each side presents its case through witnesses and exhibits. Witnesses are examined by the lawyer who called them and cross-examined by the other side; the financial records, expert reports, and deposition transcripts built up over the preceding year do their work here. The plaintiff's case goes in first, then the defendant's. You will almost certainly testify, and our preparation builds on everything that came before: the document production, the sworn Statement of Net Worth, the deposition. Composure counts. Judges watch how parties conduct themselves in the courtroom, particularly in custody cases.

How long it really takes

Even a simple divorce trial takes about five trial days, and those days are often scheduled consecutively, which is harder on your life than people expect. A block of consecutive trial days means arranging childcare and time away from work for a solid stretch you cannot move. Other times the court spreads the days out across weeks, slotted between its other matters, and each gap means re-preparing witnesses and re-orienting the court. Either way you are not in control of the calendar. A complex case, with multiple experts, contested valuations, custody proof, and evidentiary fights, can take twenty or thirty trial days or more.

Trial consumes the household that goes through it.

The Statement of Proposed Disposition

Before trial, the court's timelines require each side to file a Statement of Proposed Disposition. It sets out your positions issue by issue and tells the court exactly what you are asking it to order: how each asset should be classified and divided, what maintenance and child support should be, what the parenting arrangement should be, and why. It is a long, painstaking document, and it is often the single most important paper filed in your case. The court reads it as the roadmap of what you want and what you can prove.

Here is the practical advice that follows. Because it takes substantial attorney time to prepare, it is expensive, and the fees are unavoidable once the deadline is upon you. If your case can be settled, the right time to settle it is before that document has to be drafted. Clients who resolve their cases ahead of that deadline save real money; clients who go past it have paid for a trial document whether or not the case is ever tried. Gus will tell you when that date is coming and what it will cost, well before it arrives.

What it does to your life

You are away from work for every trial day, and effectively for the days around them. If you are a stay-at-home parent, expect to leave the house by 8:00 a.m. and not return until 5:00 or 6:00 p.m., every trial day, with childcare covered for that entire window. And the day frequently does not end at the courthouse: you will often need to stay with your lawyers into the evening preparing for the next day's witnesses. Trial consumes the household that goes through it. None of this is said to frighten you; it is said because the decision to try a case should be made by someone who knows exactly what it costs, in every currency.

The trial ends. The case does not.

The judge almost never rules from the bench. When the proof closes, the trial transcripts are ordered, which takes weeks and carries its own cost. The lawyers then submit post-trial briefs arguing what the evidence proved. And then you wait: months, commonly, for the court's written decision resolving each contested issue. That decision is reduced to a judgment of divorce, the document that legally ends the marriage and sets out every obligation: the parenting schedule, support, maintenance, and the division of property, with additional orders directing retirement plan administrators where those assets are divided.

Appeal

Either side may appeal the judgment to the Appellate Division, which for Westchester cases is the Second Department. Appeals run on strict deadlines measured from service of the judgment with notice of entry, take a year or more to resolve, and are a separate engagement outside the original retainer's scope. If there are issues worth appealing in your case, or if your spouse appeals, we will give you a candid assessment of the odds and the cost before anything is filed.
